Slab Leak Detection & Repair in Englewood, CO

A slab leak is a leak in a water line that runs under a concrete floor. Repair means locating it through the concrete, then either opening a small section of the slab to fix the pipe or abandoning that run and rerouting the line overhead. In Englewood, CO most houses have basements, so slab leaks show up most in slab-on-grade additions, garages, townhomes and basement floors with buried lines.

Signs that point under the floor

Warm floors, damp edges and a heater that never stops

Slab leaks are hard to see because the concrete hides them. People usually notice one of these first.

  • A patch of floor that feels warm under bare feet, usually a hot-water line leaking below.
  • Water seeping from the edge of a garage slab or where a slab meets the house foundation.
  • The water heater running or cycling far more than usual with no change in use.
  • Cracks in tile or flooring that appear alongside dampness.
  • A meter that keeps moving when the house is completely shut off.

If you are not sure the leak is under concrete at all, start with general leak detection. Slab repair is a separate method, so the leak's location is confirmed first.

Why slab leaks happen on the Front Range

The Colorado Geological Survey describes Colorado as one of the most severely affected areas in the nation for expansive soils. Clay that swells when wet and shrinks when dry moves concrete, and anything cast into or under it, a little every season.

The IPC (International Plumbing Code) requires pipe passing through or under concrete to be protected against corrosion and physical damage, which is why sleeving and wrapping matter.

Pipes under a slab are held tight by the concrete and soil around them. As the ground shifts, lines can rub against rebar or gravel, joints can be strained, and hot-water copper can wear through at the point where it expands and contracts. The soil under a given Englewood house varies, so we treat soil movement as a likely factor rather than a certainty.

In Englewood, slab leaks tend to show up where concrete sits directly on the ground. Typical spots are a slab-on-grade addition to an older house or an attached garage with a line to a utility sink. Townhomes and basement floors with a buried supply line are the other usual suspects.

Spot repair or reroute: comparing the options

Once the leak is located, there are two real choices. The right one depends on how old the rest of the under-slab pipe is and what sits on the floor above.

OptionHow it worksBest when
Spot repair through the slabA small section of concrete is cut out, the pipe is repaired, and the slab is patched.The pipe is otherwise sound and the floor above is easy to patch.
Reroute overheadThe leaking under-slab run is abandoned and a new line is run through walls or ceilings.The line has leaked before, or tile and cabinets make breaking the floor costly.
Full under-slab repipeAll supply lines under the slab are replaced or rerouted.Several leaks have occurred and the whole system is the same age.

A reroute usually avoids concrete work entirely, and new PEX is flexible enough to follow a practical path. For homes with repeated leaks across the whole house, a full whole-home repipe may cost less than repairing leaks one at a time.

What to do if water is coming up through a floor

Visible water from a slab is uncommon, but when it happens it needs quick, safe steps.

  • Close the main water shutoff to stop the flow feeding the leak.
  • If the leak is on the hot side, turn a gas water heater to pilot or switch off an electric one at the breaker.
  • Keep extension cords, power strips and electronics away from wet floors.
  • Lift rugs and move furniture off wet flooring to limit damage.
  • Note where the water appears first, since it helps pinpoint the leak.

How a slab leak is found without guesswork

  1. Pressure test. Hot and cold lines are tested separately to confirm which one is leaking under the slab.
  2. Acoustic listening. Ground microphones pick up the sound of water escaping through the concrete, strongest directly above the leak.
  3. Thermal imaging. A hot-water leak warms the floor above it, which a thermal camera can map.
  4. Line tracing. The pipe route is traced so the repair opening lands on the pipe, not beside it.

You see the marked spot before any cut. A typical access opening is about the size of a large floor tile.
