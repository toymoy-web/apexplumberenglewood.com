Why a Sewer Scope Matters Before You Buy an Older Englewood Home

By the Englewood Apex Plumbing team · Published October 1, 2026.

A standard home inspection looks at the plumbing you can see. It does not look inside the sewer line running under the front yard, and in Englewood that line belongs to the owner, all the way to the district main. A sewer scope, a camera run through that pipe, is one of the most useful inspections a buyer of an older Englewood home can order.

The short answer

  • Englewood homeowners own the sewer lateral from the house to the district main.
  • Most standard home inspections do not include a camera look inside it.
  • Older laterals are often clay or cast iron, prone to roots, cracks and sags.
  • Order the scope early enough to act on it within your contract deadlines.
  • Ask for the full video and the distance to each defect, along with the summary.

Why it matters in Englewood specifically

Englewood's housing grew fastest after World War II, and city-data puts the median build year for Englewood houses at 1955. Sewer laterals from that period were commonly clay tile or cast iron. Both can last a long time, but both develop predictable problems with age.

Ownership raises the stakes. South Englewood Sanitation District No. 1 puts the whole line on the owner, from the building out to and including the tap on the district main. Other districts serving Englewood follow similar rules. If the lateral under the yard is failing, the new owner pays to fix it.

A replacement lateral can run into the thousands of dollars, especially under a driveway or mature tree. A scope costs a small fraction of that and turns an unknown into a number you can negotiate with.

What the camera can show

A good sewer scope records the whole lateral from a cleanout to the main. These are the findings that matter most.

Timing it inside a Colorado contract

Colorado's standard residential purchase contract sets an inspection objection deadline, the date by which a buyer must raise inspection issues with the seller. A sewer scope is only useful if the results arrive in time to act on them, so schedule it at the same time as the general inspection, not after.

If the house is vacant, water may need to run for a while before the scope so the line is wet and debris moves. If the line is blocked, it has to be cleared before the camera can travel, which can add a visit. Ask the listing agent early where the cleanout is.

Using the results in negotiation

Buyers typically ask for one of three things when a scope shows problems: a price reduction, a seller-paid repair before closing, or a credit at closing. A written quote for the repair, based on the footage, makes any of those requests concrete. Our sewer line repair page explains the repair methods a quote might include.

What to ask for from whoever does the scope

Scope reports vary a lot. These items make one genuinely useful.

  • The full video file, rather than only still photos or a one-line summary.
  • The distance from the cleanout to each defect, so it can be located on the surface.
  • Confirmation that the camera reached the district main or an explanation of why it could not.
  • The pipe material or materials seen along the line.
  • A plain-language note on which findings are urgent and which are maintenance items.

Questions to ask the seller before the scope

The answers help you and the inspector know what to look for.

  • Has the sewer line ever backed up, and when?
  • Has the line been cleaned, lined, repaired or replaced, and is there paperwork?
  • Where is the cleanout, and is it accessible?
  • Are there large trees in the front yard or along the path of the line?
  • Has anyone else scoped the line, for example during a previous sale?

Reading between the lines of a report

A few fine roots at one joint is common in older Englewood laterals and is not a reason to walk away. It usually means regular cleaning now and a targeted repair later. Several offset joints, a long belly holding water, or a collapsed section is a different story, and it points toward a larger repair or a full replacement.

Watch for signs of a previous repair, such as a section of plastic pipe in a clay line or a liner. Those are not bad news in themselves, and they suggest the line has been maintained. Ask the seller for any paperwork on the work.

When a line is coated in grease or heavy scale, the camera may not show the pipe wall clearly. In that case, hydro jetting followed by a second camera run gives a truer picture of the pipe's condition.

When a scope is especially worth it

Some houses deserve a scope more than others. A home from the 1940s to 1970s, a lot with large trees in the front yard, a finished basement with a floor drain, or a house that has sat vacant are all good reasons. So is any sign of past trouble, such as a fresh patch of concrete in the basement floor near the stack or new sod along the path of the line.

Rentals and flips deserve extra attention. A line that was cleaned just before listing can look fine for weeks and then back up again, which is why the full video, with the pipe walls visible, matters more than a note saying the line flowed.

After you buy

Keep the video. It is a baseline for every future sewer question, and it helps a plumber compare changes years later. Note where the cleanout is and make sure it stays accessible after any landscaping.

If the scope showed roots, set a reminder to have the line cleaned before they cause a backup, typically within a year or two. If it showed structural problems you accepted in the price, plan the repair before a backup forces it. When the whole line is failing, our sewer line replacement page compares trench and trenchless options.
